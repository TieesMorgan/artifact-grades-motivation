Chapter 01 • The Unquestioned Constant

The "Grammar of Schooling"

One letter, decades of habit, almost no information.

Read the full argument

In formal schooling, grades are often treated as an unquestioned constant, part of what Tyack and Cuban (1995, as cited in Schneider & Hutt, 2014) term the “grammar of schooling.”

I initially assumed letter grades directly reflected learning, but educational research reveals a far more complex reality. Understanding what grades represent, their historical origins, and their interaction with feedback has redefined how I interpret evaluation.

Ultimately, grades function as multidimensional administrative tools rather than pure measures of learning.
Chapter 02 • Antiquity Through Renaissance

Before Numbers: Oral Disputation

Before numbers, learning was shown out loud, by arguing it live.

Read the full argument

Historically, numerical marks were not designed for pedagogical guidance. Ancient and medieval education relied on live oral disputations, dialectical questioning, and narrative feedback without letter marks or competitive rankings (Williams, 2022).

In these dialogic settings, evaluation was inseparable from instruction: instructors observed how a learner reasoned through problems in real time rather than reducing complex understanding into a permanent symbol.

Chapter 03 • 18th–19th Century Standardization

Rise of the Administrative Sorting Machine

Scarce fellowships and swelling classrooms turned ranking into a system.

Read the full argument

Standardized grading systems emerged during the 18th and 19th centuries—from Cambridge rankings to A–F scales at Harvard and Mount Holyoke—as administrative mechanisms to manage institutional records and sort students for expanding labor markets (Schneider & Hutt, 2014; Williams, 2022).

This history explains why high-stakes marking encourages students to become risk-averse “entrepreneurial learners” who optimize points over authentic curiosity (Haswell, 1999, as cited in Schneider & Hutt, 2014; Williams, 2022).

Chapter 04 • Composite Variance & Neurodivergent Equity

What a Single Letter Actually Measures

A single letter blends mastery, compliance and bias.

Read the full argument

Although students view grades as indicators of mastery, research demonstrates they are composite measures: teacher-assigned grades correlate only modestly with standardized achievement, as 75% of GPA variance reflects non-cognitive factors like effort, attendance, and subjective behavior (Brookhart et al., 2016; Williams, 2022).

Furthermore, final marks are frequently distorted by interpersonal bias and favoritism (Baniasadi et al., 2023; Brookhart et al., 2016). This vulnerability particularly affects neurodivergent learners when educators penalize behavioral differences, non-traditional problem-solving, or compliance factors like attention rather than assessing subject mastery (Arhavbarien, 2026; Baniasadi et al., 2023; Williams, 2022).

Chapter 05 • Self-Determination & Lifelong Competence

Informational Feedback Exit Ramps

Remove the letter and the feedback finally gets read.

Read the full argument

Grades and descriptive feedback influence motivation differently. Self-determination theory contrasts controlling evaluations with informational feedback that supports autonomy and competence (Ryan & Deci, 2020).

Letter grades offer minimal guidance, and combining marks with comments often leads students to ignore actionable advice (Ryan & Deci, 2020; Williams, 2022). Conversely, low-stakes practice and criteria-referenced feedback foster self-regulation without defensiveness, driving genuine intellectual growth (Arhavbarien, 2026; Baniasadi et al., 2023; Ryan & Deci, 2020).

Recognizing these limitations allows me to look past grade performance and focus on lifelong competence through low-stakes practice and informational feedback.
